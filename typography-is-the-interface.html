Typography is the interface

2026-03-07

When you strip away the buttons, the icons, the sidebar, and the toolbar — what remains is text. In a writing app, typography is the interface. Every decision about font, size, weight, and spacing directly shapes how it feels to think on screen.

Choosing a typeface

There are three families of typeface worth considering for long-form writing:

  • Serif (Playfair Display, Lora, Source Serif): The traditional choice. Serifs guide the eye along the baseline, reducing fatigue in long reading sessions. Best for editorial and literary contexts.
  • Sans-serif (Inter, Helvetica Neue, SF Pro): Clean and neutral. Works well for technical writing, UI-heavy contexts, and shorter-form content. Feels modern.
  • Monospace (JetBrains Mono, IBM Plex Mono, Fira Code): Forces every character into the same width. Excellent for code, but also creates an interesting typewriter aesthetic for personal notes.

Each choice implies a different relationship between writer and text. A serif font says "this is writing." A monospace font says "this is thinking out loud."

The weight question

Most writing apps use a single weight — regular (400). This is a mistake. You need at least three:

  1. Regular (400) for body text
  2. Medium or Semibold (500–600) for headings
  3. Bold (700) for emphasis within paragraphs

Loading more weights adds to page load time, but the typographic flexibility is worth it. A heading in font-weight: 400 looks like a mistake; a heading in font-weight: 600 looks intentional.

The space between lines

Line height is the single most impactful typographic property. Too tight and the text feels claustrophobic:

This paragraph has a line height of 1.2. Notice how the lines feel crammed together, competing for attention. Your eye struggles to track from the end of one line to the beginning of the next. Reading becomes work.

Too loose and the text loses cohesion — each line feels disconnected from its neighbors. The sweet spot depends on the typeface, but 1.7 to 1.9 works for most body text.

[typography]
body_line_height = 1.8
heading_line_height = 1.2
code_line_height = 1.6
max_width = "680px"

Measure and readability

The measure — the number of characters per line — determines how comfortable a paragraph is to read. Research consistently points to 45–75 characters as the optimal range.

On a 680px container with 17px text, you get roughly 65 characters per line. That's the sweet spot. Go wider and the reader's eye loses its place at line breaks. Go narrower and the constant line breaks feel choppy.

A note on responsive text

Fluid typography using clamp() is tempting but dangerous. A heading that looks bold at 1200px might look absurd at 375px:

/* Careful — test at every breakpoint */
h1 {
  font-size: clamp(2rem, 5vw + 1rem, 4.5rem);
  line-height: 1.1;
  letter-spacing: -0.02em;
}

The letter-spacing: -0.02em is crucial at large sizes. Without it, the characters look like they're drifting apart. At body text sizes, leave letter-spacing alone — the type designer already optimized it.

System fonts vs. web fonts

System fonts load instantly. Web fonts require a network request and may cause a flash of unstyled text (FOUT) or invisible text (FOIT). The trade-off:

ApproachProsCons
System fontsZero latency, familiarLimited selection, varies by OS
Google FontsHuge library, freeRender-blocking, privacy concerns
Self-hostedFull control, fast on repeat visitsInitial setup, larger bundle

For a published static site, Google Fonts with display=swap is the pragmatic choice. The font loads asynchronously and the system font fills in during the gap.

Good typography is invisible. The reader should never think about the font — they should just read. If the typeface calls attention to itself, something has gone wrong.

  • Verify line-height at mobile sizes
