The case for owning your tools

2026-03-07

Software used to be something you owned. You bought a box, installed a disc, and the program was yours — sitting on your hard drive, running without permission from anyone. That model is almost extinct now.

Today, most creative tools are subscriptions. Your writing lives in someone else's database. Your designs render on someone else's servers. Your notes sync through someone else's infrastructure. You don't own the tool. You rent access to it.

What we lost

The subscription model has real advantages — continuous updates, cross-device sync, lower upfront cost. But it also introduced fragilities that didn't exist before:

  • Your work is hostage to the service. If the company changes pricing, pivots, or shuts down, your data goes with it. Remember Google Reader? Sunrise Calendar? Inbox by Gmail?
  • Offline is an afterthought. Most cloud-first apps barely work without a connection. In 2024, on a plane, in a cabin, in a café with bad WiFi — you can't access your own notes.
  • Privacy is negotiable. Your content passes through servers you don't control, processed by systems you can't inspect, governed by terms of service that change without notice.

The best backup strategy is not needing one. Files on disk don't have downtime.

Local-first as a design principle

Local-first software stores data on the user's device as the primary copy. The network, if used at all, is for sync — not for functionality. The app works fully offline, and the user's data is always available, always fast, always private.

This isn't a new idea. It's the original idea — rediscovered after a decade of cloud-first excess.

The principles

The local-first manifesto outlines seven ideals:

  1. No spinners — operations are instant because they hit disk, not network
  2. Your data, your machine — no account required, no server dependency
  3. Full offline support — the network is an enhancement, not a requirement
  4. Collaboration is opt-in — sync when you want, with whom you want
  5. Longevity — files outlive apps; standards outlive companies
  6. Security by default — data never leaves the device unless you choose
  7. User control — you decide where data lives, how it's backed up, who sees it

The file system is underrated

Markdown files in a folder is not a limitation — it's a feature. Consider what the filesystem gives you for free:

CapabilityHow
Searchgrep -r "keyword" ~/notes/
BackupCopy the folder. Done.
Version controlgit init && git add .
SyncDropbox, iCloud, Syncthing, rsync
AutomationShell scripts, cron jobs, Hazel rules
PortabilityOpen in any editor, on any OS

No API, no SDK, no migration tool. Just files.

The git advantage

When your notes are files, git becomes a superpower:

# See everything you wrote last week
git log --after="1 week ago" --format="%h %s" --stat

# Find when you first mentioned a topic
git log -S "machine learning" --oneline

# Restore a deleted note
git checkout HEAD~10 -- notes/deleted-note.md

This is not hypothetical — these are real commands you can run on a Glifo vault right now. Every save creates a commit. The entire history of your thinking is preserved.

The trade-offs

Let's be honest about what local-first costs:

  • No real-time collaboration. Two people can't edit the same note simultaneously without conflict resolution (CRDTs, OT). For a personal tool, this is fine. For a team wiki, it's a dealbreaker.
  • Sync is manual. You push and pull explicitly. There's no magic background sync that "just works" across devices.
  • Backup is your responsibility. If your disk dies and you haven't pushed to a remote, that's on you.

These are real trade-offs. But for personal writing — journals, notes, drafts, essays — the benefits overwhelmingly outweigh the costs.

The tools you use shape the thoughts you have. Fragile tools produce anxious thinking. Reliable tools produce focused thinking. Own your tools and your thoughts stay yours.
