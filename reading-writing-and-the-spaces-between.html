Reading, writing, and the spaces between

2026-03-07

Every writer has rituals. Some write at dawn, some at midnight. Some need silence; others need noise. But every writer agrees on one thing: the tool should disappear. The moment you notice the cursor, the font, the margins — you've lost the thread.

What good writers read

The best writing advice is not about writing at all — it's about reading. Here are the books that shaped how I think about craft:

  1. On Writing Well by William Zinsser — The case for simplicity. Every sentence should earn its place. Every word should earn its letters.
  2. Bird by Bird by Anne Lamott — Permission to write badly. The first draft is supposed to be terrible. That's what revision is for.
  3. Several Short Sentences About Writing by Verlyn Klinkenborg — A radical rethinking of how sentences work. Short. Direct. Every sentence a complete thought.
  4. The Elements of Style by Strunk & White — Controversial now, but still the fastest way to learn what not to do.

Write with nouns and verbs, not with adjectives and adverbs. The adjective hasn't been built that can pull a weak or inaccurate noun out of a tight place.

— Strunk & White

The daily practice

Writing is a muscle. Like any muscle, it atrophies without use. A daily practice doesn't have to be ambitious:

  • Write for 20 minutes before checking email
  • Keep a single running note for the day's thoughts
  • Don't edit while writing — that's a different mode of thinking
  • Review yesterday's notes before starting today's
  • End each session mid-thought — it makes starting tomorrow easier

Morning pages

Julia Cameron's morning pages technique is deceptively simple: write three pages, longhand, first thing in the morning. No planning, no editing, no audience. The practice works because:

  • It clears the mental buffer — anxieties, to-dos, random noise
  • It builds the writing habit without performance pressure
  • It occasionally surfaces ideas you didn't know you had

The pages are not meant to be art. They are not even meant to be writing. They are meant to be done.

On structure

Good writing has rhythm. Short sentences create urgency. Long sentences, with their subordinate clauses and careful qualifications, create space for nuance and complexity. The best paragraphs alternate between the two.

Consider these nested ideas:

  • Structure
    • Macro structure: the arc of the whole piece
    • Micro structure: the rhythm of sentences within a paragraph
      • Sentence length variation
      • Paragraph length variation
      • Strategic use of fragments
    • Visual structure: how the text looks on the page
      • White space as punctuation
      • Headings as signposts
      • Lists as decompression zones

This hierarchy is invisible in finished writing, but it's the skeleton that holds everything upright.

The paragraph as a unit of thought

A paragraph is not "a bunch of sentences about the same thing." It is a unit of thought — a single idea, developed and resolved. When you feel the urge to start a new paragraph, you're not just adding a line break. You're telling the reader: "That thought is complete. Here comes a new one."

The ideal paragraph length for screen reading is 3–5 sentences. Shorter than that and the text feels scattered. Longer and the reader's eye needs a break it isn't getting.

Tools for thinking

The best note-taking apps understand that writing is thinking — not transcription. Features that support thinking:

FeatureWhy it matters
WikilinksConnect ideas across notes without hierarchy
BacklinksSee which notes reference the current one
Quick searchFind thoughts faster than you forget them
Version historyThinking evolves; early drafts have value
Distraction-free modeRemove everything except the words

None of these features are about formatting. They're about navigating thought — finding connections, preserving context, reducing friction between having an idea and capturing it.

The best writing tool is the one you forget you're using. It should feel like thinking, not like operating software.
